The many faces of importance sampling

I’m a data science student, and as part of the degree we choose a specialization. I chose finance. Last semester, I took Financial Engineering and Numerical Methods for Finance, which included pricing derivatives. Both were tough: I had to learn a lot of stochastic calculus that was new to me, but I found it really interesting. We covered variance reduction for Monte Carlo simulation and Girsanov’s theorem.

I also encountered importance sampling in Probabilistic AI (PAI). In June 2026, I attended the Reinforcement Learning Summer School (RLSS 2026), which inspired the reinforcement learning part of this article.

Here, I want to bring these examples together in one place and explain the intuition behind them, as I understand it. The connection I find useful is that we can assign different probabilities to the same possible outcomes and use weights to account for that change. I’ll start with this idea, then work through the examples to show how they relate to one another.

Importance weights

One way to visualize probability is to imagine a random experiment branching into many possible worlds.

Each world represents a possible outcome, and a probability distribution determines how likely that world is.

Now imagine describing the same collection of worlds using two different probability distributions, ppp and qqq.

Suppose some outcome xxx is three times as likely under ppp as under qqq. If we observe that outcome while sampling from qqq, we can think of it as representing three times as much probability mass under ppp.

This gives us the importance weight:

w(x)=p(x)q(x).w(x)=\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}.w(x)=q(x)p(x)​.

An outcome with weight three counts three times as much.

An outcome with weight one-half counts half as much.

This intuition becomes precise when we consider expectations. Suppose we want to compute the expectation of a function fff under distribution ppp:

Ep[f(X)]=∫f(x)p(x) dx.\mathbb E_p[f(X)]=\int f(x)p(x)\,dx.Ep​[f(X)]=∫f(x)p(x)dx.

By multiplying and dividing by another density q(x)q(x)q(x), we obtain

Ep[f(X)]=Eq[p(X)q(X)f(X)].\mathbb E_p[f(X)] = \mathbb E_q\left[ \frac{p(X)}{q(X)}f(X) \right].Ep​[f(X)]=Eq​[q(X)p(X)​f(X)].

Instead of sampling from ppp, we can sample from qqq and correct each observation using its probability ratio, provided qqq covers all the relevant outcomes.

There is a more general mathematical principle hiding behind this identity. In measure theory, the density ratio becomes the Radon–Nikodym derivative:

EP[F]=EQ[dPdQF].\mathbb E_P[F] = \mathbb E_Q\left[ \frac{dP}{dQ}F \right].EP​[F]=EQ​[dQdP​F].

Here, PPP and QQQ are probability measures, and PPP must be absolutely continuous with respect to QQQ.

This formula is the common thread running through all our examples. The interesting part is how differently we can use it.

Monte Carlo variance reduction

Let's start with Monte Carlo simulation.

Suppose we're estimating the probability of an event that happens only occasionally. If we sample directly from the original distribution, most observations might tell us very little about that event.

Consider a distribution with two Gaussian peaks:

p(x)=0.95 N(−2,0.62)+0.05 N(2,0.62).p(x)=0.95\,\mathcal N(-2,0.6^2) +0.05\,\mathcal N(2,0.6^2).p(x)=0.95N(−2,0.62)+0.05N(2,0.62).

Most of its probability mass lies near −2-2−2, while only a small portion lies near 222.

Now suppose we want to estimate

P(X>0).P(X>0).P(X>0).

With ordinary Monte Carlo, we draw observations from ppp and count how many are greater than zero.

Out of 1,000 samples, only about 50 are expected to fall into this region. The remaining 950 contribute zero to our estimate.

What if we could deliberately visit the right-hand peak more frequently?

Instead of sampling from ppp, consider

q(x)=0.5 N(−2,0.62)+0.5 N(2,0.62).q(x)=0.5\,\mathcal N(-2,0.6^2) +0.5\,\mathcal N(2,0.6^2).q(x)=0.5N(−2,0.62)+0.5N(2,0.62).

We've increased the probability of visiting the right-hand peak from approximately 5% to 50%.

Suddenly, around 500 observations out of 1,000 contribute to our estimate.

Of course, we've artificially made the event more common. Simply counting these observations would give us the wrong answer.

But importance sampling fixes this. Near the right-hand peak, each observation receives a weight of approximately

w(x)≈0.050.5=0.1.w(x)\approx\frac{0.05}{0.5}=0.1.w(x)≈0.50.05​=0.1.

We now encounter the important worlds ten times as frequently, but each carries approximately one-tenth of its previous contribution.

The expected value stays unchanged.

Variance of the estimator

The key is that we've made the individual contributions less erratic.

To see this, let's temporarily treat our two Gaussian peaks as nonoverlapping.

Under ordinary Monte Carlo, an observation contributes either 1, with probability 0.05, or 0, with probability 0.95.

The variance of this contribution is

Var⁡p(f(X))=0.05(0.95)=0.0475.\operatorname{Var}_p(f(X)) =0.05(0.95)=0.0475.Varp​(f(X))=0.05(0.95)=0.0475.

Under importance sampling, an observation contributes approximately 0.1 with probability 0.5, or 0 otherwise.

Its variance is

Var⁡q(wf)=0.5(0.1)2−0.052=0.0025.\operatorname{Var}_q(wf) =0.5(0.1)^2-0.05^2 =0.0025.Varq​(wf)=0.5(0.1)2−0.052=0.0025.

The ratio is

0.04750.0025=19.\frac{0.0475}{0.0025}=19.0.00250.0475​=19.

So in this simplified example, importance sampling reduces the variance by a factor of 19. Accounting for the small overlap between the two Gaussian components gives a factor of approximately 17.7.

The intuition is simple: instead of observing many zeros and occasionally a large contribution, we observe smaller contributions much more frequently.

This suggests a general principle. For estimating Ep[f(X)]\mathbb E_p[f(X)]Ep​[f(X)], the theoretically optimal proposal is proportional to

q∗(x)∝p(x)∣f(x)∣.q^*(x)\propto p(x)|f(x)|.q∗(x)∝p(x)∣f(x)∣.

We should assign more sampling probability to outcomes that contribute strongly to our expectation.

For nonnegative fff, this ideal proposal even gives zero variance: every weighted contribution is identical. Unfortunately, constructing it generally requires knowing an unknown normalizing constant. For nonnegative fff, that constant is precisely the expectation we're trying to calculate.

Nevertheless, the principle remains useful:

importance sampling works well when it makes weighted contributions more consistent.

But what happens when we don't get to choose our sampling distribution?

Off-policy reinforcement learning

In reinforcement learning, the motivation for importance sampling is often quite different.

Imagine an agent interacting with an environment. It takes actions, receives rewards, and generates trajectories consisting of states and actions.

These trajectories were collected using some behavior policy bbb. However, we're interested in evaluating another policy, π\piπ.

We could collect new trajectories using π\piπ, but what if interacting with the environment is expensive? Can we reuse the trajectories we already have?

Once again, importance sampling provides an answer.

A trajectory τ\tauτ is now one of our possible worlds. Its probability depends on the policy generating the actions and the environment's transition dynamics.

The importance weight for a trajectory is

w(τ)=Pπ(τ)Pb(τ).w(\tau)=\frac{P_\pi(\tau)}{P_b(\tau)}.w(τ)=Pb​(τ)Pπ​(τ)​.

Assuming the environment dynamics and initial-state distribution are unchanged, their probabilities cancel in the ratio, leaving

w(τ)=∏t=0T−1π(at∣st)b(at∣st).w(\tau)= \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} \frac{\pi(a_t\mid s_t)} {b(a_t\mid s_t)}.w(τ)=t=0∏T−1​b(at​∣st​)π(at​∣st​)​.

Notice that the weight is a product of probability ratios. That's because a trajectory consists of successive decisions, and its probability is built from the conditional probabilities of those decisions.

A two-step trajectory example

Consider a simple two-step environment.

At each step, the agent chooses between actions A and B.

The behavior policy chooses A with probability 1/41/41/4, whereas the target policy chooses A with probability 3/43/43/4.

Suppose the agent receives a reward of 1 only if it chooses A at both steps.

There are four possible action trajectories: AA, AB, BA, and BB.

Under the behavior policy, the probability of AA is

Pb(AA)=14⋅14=116.P_b(AA)=\frac14\cdot\frac14=\frac1{16}.Pb​(AA)=41​⋅41​=161​.

Under the target policy,

Pπ(AA)=34⋅34=916.P_\pi(AA)=\frac34\cdot\frac34=\frac9{16}.Pπ​(AA)=43​⋅43​=169​.

Therefore,

w(AA)=9.w(AA)=9.w(AA)=9.

This is where our possible-worlds interpretation becomes useful.

Imagine dividing the probability space into sixteen equally sized pieces. Under the behavior policy, AA occupies one piece. Under the target policy, it occupies nine.

One AA trajectory observed under the behavior policy therefore represents nine times as much probability under the target policy.

And because AA is the only trajectory receiving a reward, we recover the target policy's expected return:

J(π)=Eb[w(τ)G(τ)]=116⋅9=916.\begin{aligned} J(\pi) &=\mathbb E_b[w(\tau)G(\tau)]\\ &=\frac1{16}\cdot9\\ &=\frac9{16}. \end{aligned}J(π)​=Eb​[w(τ)G(τ)]=161​⋅9=169​.​

The interesting difference from our Monte Carlo example is that we haven't chosen a new distribution to reduce variance.

Instead, we're correcting for the distribution that generated our existing data.

This distinction also explains a major challenge in off-policy reinforcement learning.

If the behavior policy rarely generates trajectories that are common under the target policy, their importance weights can become enormous. Over long trajectories, multiplying many probability ratios can make estimates extremely unstable.

Importance ratios in PPO

A related idea appears in Proximal Policy Optimization, or PPO.

PPO collects trajectories using an older policy and then performs several optimization steps to improve it. Its objective contains the probability ratio

rt(θ)=πθ(at∣st)πold(at∣st).r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)} {\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)}.rt​(θ)=πold​(at​∣st​)πθ​(at​∣st​)​.

This ratio measures how much more or less likely an observed action becomes under the updated policy.

PPO uses it inside a clipped surrogate objective, discouraging overly large policy updates.

Unlike ordinary off-policy Monte Carlo, PPO is not constructing an unbiased full-trajectory importance-sampling estimator. But the same probability-ratio mechanism is central to its optimization.

And there's an interesting connection waiting here: in reinforcement learning, we reweight discrete trajectories. What would the equivalent operation look like for continuous stochastic paths?

Girsanov’s theorem

This brings us to Girsanov's theorem.

Consider standard Brownian motion under a probability measure PPP:

dXt=dWtP.dX_t=dW_t^P.dXt​=dWtP​.

Suppose we're interested in the probability that the process ends above 2 after one unit of time:

P(X1>2)≈0.0228.P(X_1>2)\approx0.0228.P(X1​>2)≈0.0228.

Only about 2.3% of simulated paths satisfy this condition.

Just as in our first Monte Carlo example, we could make the event more common by introducing a drift.

Let's consider another probability measure QQQ, under which

dXt=2 dt+dWtQ.dX_t=2\,dt+dW_t^Q.dXt​=2dt+dWtQ​.

Now X1∼N(2,1)X_1\sim\mathcal N(2,1)X1​∼N(2,1), so

Q(X1>2)=0.5.Q(X_1>2)=0.5.Q(X1​>2)=0.5.

Half the paths end above the threshold.

But these paths are distributed according to QQQ, while our original question concerns PPP.

How should we reweight them?

Deriving the path weight

Instead of starting with Girsanov's theorem, let's look at what happens over a tiny time interval.

Under PPP, a Brownian increment has distribution

ΔXt∼N(0,Δt).\Delta X_t\sim\mathcal N(0,\Delta t).ΔXt​∼N(0,Δt).

Under QQQ, with constant drift uuu,

ΔXt∼N(uΔt,Δt).\Delta X_t\sim\mathcal N(u\Delta t,\Delta t).ΔXt​∼N(uΔt,Δt).

Taking the ratio of the two Gaussian densities gives

p(Δx)q(Δx)=exp⁡(−uΔx+12u2Δt).\frac{p(\Delta x)}{q(\Delta x)} = \exp\left( -u\Delta x+\frac12u^2\Delta t \right).q(Δx)p(Δx)​=exp(−uΔx+21​u2Δt).

Now think back to reinforcement learning.

There, the weight of an entire trajectory was obtained by multiplying the probability ratios of successive actions.

Here, we do essentially the same thing with Gaussian increments.

Multiplying these ratios across a discretized path gives

w(τ)=∏tp(Δxt)q(Δxt)=exp⁡(−u∑tΔxt+12u2∑tΔt).\begin{aligned} w(\tau) &=\prod_t\frac{p(\Delta x_t)}{q(\Delta x_t)}\\ &=\exp\left( -u\sum_t\Delta x_t +\frac12u^2\sum_t\Delta t \right). \end{aligned}w(τ)​=t∏​q(Δxt​)p(Δxt​)​=exp(−ut∑​Δxt​+21​u2t∑​Δt).​

Since the increments sum to XTX_TXT​ when X0=0X_0=0X0​=0, we obtain

w(τ)=exp⁡(−uXT+12u2T).w(\tau) = \exp\left(-uX_T+\frac12u^2T\right).w(τ)=exp(−uXT​+21​u2T).

For u=2u=2u=2 and T=1T=1T=1,

dPdQ=exp⁡(−2X1+2).\frac{dP}{dQ} = \exp(-2X_1+2).dQdP​=exp(−2X1​+2).

This is exactly the likelihood ratio that Girsanov's theorem gives in our constant-drift example.

We can therefore calculate

P(X1>2)=EQ[1{X1>2}dPdQ].P(X_1>2) = \mathbb E_Q\left[ \mathbf1_{\{X_1>2\}} \frac{dP}{dQ} \right].P(X1​>2)=EQ​[1{X1​>2}​dQdP​].

The same importance-sampling identity has appeared again.

The only difference is that we're now working with probability measures on continuous stochastic paths rather than discrete action sequences.

For more general drift processes, the likelihood ratio becomes a stochastic exponential involving integrals with respect to Brownian motion. Girsanov's theorem formalizes this change of measure under suitable conditions.

Risk-neutral pricing

In mathematical finance, Girsanov's theorem takes on another role.

Consider a stock price modeled under the real-world measure PPP:

dSt=μSt dt+σSt dWtP.dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t^P.dSt​=μSt​dt+σSt​dWtP​.

Here, μ\muμ is the real-world drift and σ\sigmaσ is volatility.

For derivative pricing, we introduce a risk-neutral measure QQQ, under which the stock follows, in a simple no-dividend model,

dSt=rSt dt+σSt dWtQ,dS_t = rS_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t^Q,dSt​=rSt​dt+σSt​dWtQ​,

where rrr is the risk-free interest rate.

How does this change happen?

Define the market price of risk as

λ=μ−rσ.\lambda=\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma}.λ=σμ−r​.

Girsanov allows us to introduce a Brownian motion under QQQ such that

dWtQ=dWtP+λ dt.dW_t^Q=dW_t^P+\lambda\,dt.dWtQ​=dWtP​+λdt.

Substituting dWtP=dWtQ−λ dtdW_t^P=dW_t^Q-\lambda\,dtdWtP​=dWtQ​−λdt into the original stock-price equation gives

dSt=μSt dt+σSt(dWtQ−λ dt)=(μ−σλ)St dt+σSt dWtQ=rSt dt+σSt dWtQ.\begin{aligned} dS_t &=\mu S_t\,dt +\sigma S_t(dW_t^Q-\lambda\,dt)\\ &=(\mu-\sigma\lambda)S_t\,dt +\sigma S_t\,dW_t^Q\\ &=rS_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,dW_t^Q. \end{aligned}dSt​​=μSt​dt+σSt​(dWtQ​−λdt)=(μ−σλ)St​dt+σSt​dWtQ​=rSt​dt+σSt​dWtQ​.​

The real-world drift μ\muμ has become the risk-free rate rrr.

Under the risk-neutral measure, discounted traded asset prices are martingales, allowing us to price a replicable European payoff H(ST)H(S_T)H(ST​) through

V0=e−rTEQ[H(ST)].V_0=e^{-rT}\mathbb E_Q[H(S_T)].V0​=e−rTEQ​[H(ST​)].

Here, we're not changing measures to obtain a more efficient Monte Carlo estimator.

We're changing measures to satisfy the economic conditions underlying no-arbitrage pricing.

Yet the mathematical operation is still the same: a Radon–Nikodym derivative relates expectations under two probability measures.

Limits and weight degeneracy

At this point, importance sampling might seem almost too powerful.

We can choose where we sample, reuse trajectories collected under another policy, and even move between probability measures on stochastic paths.

But there are limitations.

First, our sampling measure must assign positive probability wherever the target measure assigns probability to outcomes relevant to our expectation. Otherwise, we cannot recover contributions from outcomes that are impossible to sample.

Second, importance weights can become extremely uneven.

If an outcome is common under the target distribution but exceptionally rare under the sampling distribution, its importance weight becomes enormous.

A small number of observations may then dominate the entire estimate. This is known as weight degeneracy.

The issue is particularly severe in long-horizon reinforcement learning, where trajectory weights are products of many ratios. Similar problems can arise when changing measures over continuous stochastic paths.

So although importance sampling preserves expectations under appropriate conditions, it does not necessarily preserve—or improve—the precision of an estimator.

Changing probability measures is powerful, but choosing or working with those measures still requires care.

How the applications connect

We've now seen the same mathematical principle appearing in four different settings.

In Monte Carlo simulation, we use importance sampling to explore important regions of a distribution more frequently and reduce variance.

In reinforcement learning, we use it to reason about policies using trajectories generated by other policies.

For continuous stochastic processes, Girsanov's theorem turns a change of drift into a likelihood ratio on entire paths.

And in mathematical finance, changes of probability measure allow us to move from real-world dynamics to risk-neutral valuation.

These applications aren't merely connected by a similar-looking formula. They are instances of the same underlying mathematical identity:

EP[F]=EQ[dPdQF].\mathbb E_P[F] = \mathbb E_Q\left[ \frac{dP}{dQ}F \right].EP​[F]=EQ​[dQdP​F].

The surprising part isn't that the same ratio appears in different fields. It's that changing probability measures can solve fundamentally different problems: inefficient simulation, unavailable on-policy data, and the valuation of uncertain future payoffs.

The mathematics is shared; the reasons for using it are not.

And perhaps that's the most useful way to think about importance sampling: the possible worlds don't have to change. Sometimes, all we need to change is how much each world counts.
